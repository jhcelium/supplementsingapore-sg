# VNS List Check

A VNS list check helps Singapore supplement shoppers understand what HSA's voluntary notification list can and cannot tell them. It is a useful public-reference step, but it is not a medical, safety, quality, or authenticity guarantee. This article is educational information, not medical advice.

## What the VNS list is for

HSA's voluntary notification pages cover health supplements, traditional medicines, medicated oils, balms, and medicated plasters. The public list is meant to show products that companies have voluntarily notified to HSA based on declarations that they comply with published Complementary Health Products guidelines.

For a buyer, that makes the page a useful starting point for a name check, not an endpoint for trust.

## What a match does not prove

A product appearing in a notification list should not be read as "HSA-approved." HSA's list page says notified products should not be misconstrued as endorsement, and the products are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent disease. That distinction is important because ordinary health supplements are not the same as registered therapeutic medicines.

## Use a five-step check

1. Search the product name and brand name on HSA's list page.
2. Compare the exact label name, dosage form, and responsible company details where visible.
3. Read the seller's claim wording for disease, cure, prevention, or guaranteed-result language.
4. Check the actual bottle or listing for batch number, expiry date, ingredient list, and contactable seller.
5. If anything feels inconsistent, pause and check HSA alerts or ask a pharmacist before buying.

This process is intentionally conservative. A list match may reduce one uncertainty, but it does not answer every question a shopper has about quality, suitability, or the seller's copy.

## Why the process is voluntary

HSA's VNS process page describes who can submit a notification, documents required, fees, submission route, and turnaround time. That makes the notification system more transparent for companies and easier for shoppers to understand. It also means the page is not a personal recommendation engine. The notification reflects information submitted through the voluntary pathway.

For Singapore shoppers, the practical question is not "Is this list good or bad?" It is "What does this list tell me, and what must I still verify?" The answer is usually: use the list to check whether a product appears in HSA's public notification context, then continue checking claims, label completeness, seller accountability, and personal suitability.

## When to slow down

Slow down if the seller uses phrases such as "HSA-approved," "clinically proven supplement," "treats," "cures," "prevents," or fast-result disease wording. HSA's health supplement claims guidance restricts medicinal claims for this category. Strong claims are especially risky when paired with overseas checkout pages, limited label photos, or a seller who cannot provide batch and expiry details.

For people who are pregnant, taking medicine, managing a diagnosed condition, preparing for surgery, or buying for a child or older adult, a public list search is not enough. Ask a qualified doctor or pharmacist before using a supplement.

## FAQ

**Does a VNS listing mean a supplement is HSA-approved?** No. HSA's notified-product list should not be read as an endorsement. Treat it as a public notification signal only.

**Should I buy a supplement only because it appears on the list?** No. Still check the full label, seller identity, batch number, expiry date, and claim wording before deciding.

**What if the product is not on the list?** Do not assume it is unsafe based only on absence from the list. Use other checks, including HSA alerts, label completeness, and professional advice when the product relates to personal health.

**Can a notified supplement claim to treat a disease?** No. Health supplement claims still need to stay within allowed supplement language and must not become treatment, prevention, or cure claims.
