# Supplement Red Flag Map

A supplement red flag map helps Singapore readers decide which public checks to make before comparing price, seller claims, or imported product listings. This article is educational information, not medical advice, and it does not decide whether any specific supplement is safe or suitable for you.

## Start with the public rule layer

HSA's public health-supplement pages are the first layer because they define the category and the claim boundaries. The overview page explains health supplements as products used to supplement the diet and support or maintain healthy body functions. The claims page and official claims guideline keep the language away from disease treatment, prevention, cure, diagnosis, or outcome-promise wording.

## Match the red flag to the right check

Different red flags need different evidence. A claim problem is not the same as a seller transparency problem, and a VNS listing is not the same as a product recommendation. Use this table as a route map, not as a pass-or-fail score.

| Red flag seen by a buyer | First public check | Safer interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Disease, cure, prevention, or outcome-promise wording | HSA claims page and claims guideline | Treat as claim-risk language and slow down |
| "Clinically proven" or "proven by clinical trials" on a supplement ad | HSA claims guideline and claims list | Do not treat the phrase as automatic proof |
| Seller says "HSA-approved supplement" | HSA overview, VNS pages, and site definitions | Check whether the wording confuses notification, rules, and approval |
| Product name appears on a VNS list | HSA VNS list and VNS process page | Treat as a notification signal, not an endorsement |
| Imported listing has weak label photos | Label and seller checks before price comparison | Ask for full label, batch, expiry, and responsible seller details |

## Use VNS as one signal, not the whole answer

HSA's voluntary notification pages are useful when a buyer wants to understand whether a health supplement or traditional medicine appears in a public notification context. The caution is interpretation. A public listing can help with a name check, but it should not replace label review, seller accountability, or personal health advice.

This is why the VNS route belongs in the middle of the map. It may reduce one uncertainty, but it does not prove authenticity, suitability, ingredient quality, or responsible advertising. If a product is for a child, an older adult, a pregnant person, someone taking medicine, or someone managing a diagnosed condition, pause and ask a qualified doctor or pharmacist.

## Run a five-step buyer screen

1. Identify the product category: vitamin, mineral, botanical, probiotic, protein, or another supplement type.
2. Read the claim verbs: support, maintain, treat, prevent, cure, reverse, prove, or guarantee.
3. Compare the exact product name and responsible seller details against public HSA information where relevant.
4. Ask for the full label, ingredient list, batch number, expiry date, and local contact details where available.
5. If a claim or label detail feels unclear, slow down before paying or sharing the product with family.

This checklist is intentionally conservative. It cannot prove personal suitability, but it helps a reader avoid treating a marketplace listing, influencer post, or search result as a complete safety review.

## FAQ

**Is this map a medical safety check?** No. It is a public-information route map for Singapore supplement buyers. It is not medical advice, diagnosis, treatment, legal advice, or a product recommendation.

**Does a VNS listing mean a product is endorsed?** No. Treat a VNS match as one public notification signal. Continue checking claims, label completeness, seller identity, batch details, expiry details, and personal suitability.

**What is the biggest wording red flag?** Disease-style wording is the main red flag. Claims about treatment, prevention, cure, diagnosis, or similar medical outcomes need caution in health-supplement contexts.

**Should I ignore a supplement if it has no public alert?** No. Absence of a public alert is not the same as proof of safety, authenticity, or suitability. Use it as only one part of a broader screen.
