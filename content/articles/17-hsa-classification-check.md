# HSA Classification Check

An HSA classification check helps you organise a Singapore product question before deciding whether it belongs with health supplements, food, or another regulatory route. This guide explains the public tools and the information they require. It is educational information, not medical or legal advice, and it does not classify a specific product.

## Start with the question the tools can answer

Classification is about the product as presented, not simply the ingredient name printed largest on the pack. HSA's Complementary Health Products (CHP) Classification Tool describes itself as a quick guide for typical complementary health products. The official classification Q&A says the relevant factors include ingredients, claims about purpose or benefit, directions for use, and dosage form.

That means a capsule and a drink with a similar highlighted ingredient may not automatically follow the same route. The official food-health classification tree also says it is guidance and recommends seeking confirmation from the relevant authority when classification remains uncertain. Treat the self-check as a routing aid, not an approval mark or product recommendation.

## Build a source record before starting

Original element 1, captured on 13 July 2026: the live [CHP Classification Tool](https://selfhelp.hsa.gov.sg/hsa/chpb) asks users to prepare five information groups before beginning. Record them from the actual label or manufacturer information rather than filling gaps from memory:

1. Names and quantities of ingredients.
2. Scientific names and plant parts for botanical ingredients.
3. Source materials for vitamins and minerals.
4. Intended use and the exact claim wording.
5. Dosage form, directions, and measurable serving details.

Keep label photos and the source page beside this record. If the botanical part, ingredient quantity, or intended use is missing, the tool itself directs users to check with the manufacturer or supplier. A confident classification answer should not be built on an incomplete label.

## Compare the main routing signals

Original element 2, captured on 13 July 2026: this route table combines the official [food-health classification tree](https://file.go.gov.sg/hsa-food-health-product-class-tree.pdf), the [classification Q&A](https://file.go.gov.sg/hsa-tool-qna.pdf), and the [Health Supplements Guidelines](https://file.go.gov.sg/hsa-hs-guidelines.pdf). It separates three signals that buyers often blend together.

| Signal to record | What the official material distinguishes | Practical next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | Part of a daily diet, supplementation to a diet, or medicinal purpose | Copy the exact intended-use and claim words |
| Presentation | Conventional food presentation or pharmaceutical presentation | Record whether it is a drink, bar, loose powder, tablet, capsule, softgel, or another form |
| Dosing pattern | No defined dose versus small unit or defined daily doses | Record serving size, frequency, and any restrictions |
| Ingredient detail | Active ingredients, quantities, sources, and botanical parts | Resolve missing facts with the manufacturer or supplier |

These are routing signals, not a shopper score. For example, the classification Q&A notes that powder may overlap with food presentation, so overall appearance, claims, packaging, and formulation may need consideration together.

## Run a six-step classification check

1. Photograph the front, ingredient panel, directions, warnings, and responsible-company details.
2. Transcribe the ingredient names and quantities without simplifying botanical or chemical names.
3. Copy the intended-use and benefit claims exactly, including words on online listings.
4. Record the dosage form, measurable dose, frequency, and whether the product resembles a conventional food.
5. Use the CHP tool with the recorded facts and save or print the outcome for your notes.
6. If the facts do not fit the tool or remain ambiguous, use the relevant authority's official contact route rather than guessing.

Do not convert a tool outcome into wording such as "HSA approved." HSA's January 2026 guidelines state that health supplements are not subject to premarket approval and licensing by HSA for importation, manufacture, and sales in Singapore.

## Know where the self-check stops

A classification route does not establish authenticity, manufacturing quality, individual suitability, or the truth of every advertising claim. It also does not replace a professional review of personal health questions. The official guidelines say HSA reserves the right to determine final product classification, while the classification tree says the responsible agency should be consulted when uncertainty remains.

Pause if the label and seller page conflict, the ingredient quantity is absent, a botanical part is unclear, or disease-style claims appear. Keep classification, claim review, label review, and seller verification as separate checks. For medicines, diagnosed conditions, pregnancy, surgery, children, older adults, or possible interactions, ask a qualified doctor or pharmacist rather than relying on a web tool.

## FAQ

**Does the CHP tool approve a supplement?** No. It is a quick classification guide. A tool outcome is not an HSA approval, endorsement, authenticity finding, or personal suitability assessment.

**Is a powder always a health supplement?** No. HSA's classification Q&A notes that powder can overlap with food presentation. Purpose, claims, packaging, formulation, and directions may all matter.

**Can I classify a botanical product from the common name alone?** Not reliably. The live tool asks for scientific names and plant parts, while its Q&A explains that botanical ingredient options may distinguish specific plant parts.

**What if the label does not provide enough information?** Ask the manufacturer or supplier for the missing facts. If the route remains uncertain, consult the relevant authority using its official channel.
